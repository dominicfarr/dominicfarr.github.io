# Hiring rubric, levelled

From the How I Hire series (domfarr.com), part 3: Vet.

Today's version. At Policy Expert the rubric was pass/fail per competency; the 1–4 grading and the three levels came later.

How to use it: each panellist scores the take-home and the face-to-face session independently, one score per competency, before any discussion. Then the panel votes at the same moment, thumbs up or thumbs down. One thumbs down is a no, hiring manager included.

Where each competency is seen: problem-solving & code quality (take-home + face to face); technical judgment (face to face: "how would you add X?"); communication & collaboration (every stage); curiosity & growth (face to face: "something outside your main role").

## Junior

| Competency | 1 – Does not meet | 2 – Partially meets | 3 – Meets | 4 – Exceeds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem-solving & code quality** | Starts coding without understanding the task; code doesn't run or can't be followed | Gets something working with heavy prompting; structure is accidental | Asks a clarifying question or two; code works and is readable; with guidance, surfaces the constraints and handles the obvious edge cases | Breaks the problem down without prompting; code is readable with sensible structure; handles edge cases they found themselves |
| **Technical judgment** | Can't say why they did what they did | Describes what they did; "it worked" is the reason | With guidance, explains a choice and can name what else they considered | Explains their choices, names an alternative and why they rejected it; spots a way it could fail |
| **Communication & collaboration** | Disengaged or talks past the question | Answers when asked, but you pull the reasoning out | Talks through their thinking when prompted; asks for help in a useful way; takes feedback and uses it | Explains their thinking clearly while working; asks good questions; acts on feedback straight away |
| **Curiosity & growth** | No interest beyond the task in front of them | Has learned things, but only when they had to | Has taught themselves something and can say what they did with it | Has gone looking for adjacent problems and has a concrete example of learning outside their lane and applying it |

## Mid

| Competency | 1 – Does not meet | 2 – Partially meets | 3 – Meets | 4 – Exceeds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem-solving & code quality** | Jumps in without clarifying; code is hard to follow; no edge cases considered | Works but needs prompting to surface constraints; code is functional but unstructured | Clarifies the problem, decomposes naturally, code is readable with sensible structure; handles edge cases they identified | Identifies implicit constraints unprompted; code is clean, modular, testable; proactively names trade-offs and alternatives |
| **Technical judgment** | Can't explain why their approach works; no awareness of trade-offs | Describes what they did but can't articulate why; "it worked" | Explains their choices, names at least one alternative and why they rejected it; identifies a failure mode | Connects decisions to operational concerns (scale, maintainability, team skill); discusses how they'd evolve the design; names what they'd change in hindsight |
| **Communication & collaboration** | Disengaged, monosyllabic, or talks past the question; code has no comments or structure | Communicates but you have to pull out the reasoning; code is readable but not self-explanatory | Explains their thinking clearly while working; code is self-documenting; asks good questions; accepts and acts on feedback | Proactively structures their explanation for the audience; writes code that a new teammate could pick up; gives and receives feedback constructively in the live discussion |
| **Curiosity & growth** | Narrow focus; no interest beyond the immediate task; no examples of learning something new | Has learned new things but reactively (when forced); limited examples | Actively seeks out adjacent problems; has a concrete example of learning something outside their lane and applying it | Drives improvement beyond their role; has mentored or influenced others; connects their work to a larger system or goal |

## Senior

| Competency | 1 – Does not meet | 2 – Partially meets | 3 – Meets | 4 – Exceeds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem-solving & code quality** | Solves the task but misses constraints a senior should see; structure won't survive the next requirement | Clean, readable code, but trade-offs only come out when asked | Identifies implicit constraints unprompted; code is clean, modular, testable; names trade-offs and alternatives without being asked | Shapes the problem itself: questions the requirement where it should be questioned; the design makes the next change cheap, and they can show why |
| **Technical judgment** | Explains choices locally but can't connect them to how the system runs or changes | Names alternatives, but decisions aren't tied to scale, maintainability or the team | Connects decisions to operational concerns (scale, maintainability, team skill); discusses how they'd evolve the design; names what they'd change in hindsight | Weighs decisions against the team and the business as well as the code; knows when good enough is right and says so |
| **Communication & collaboration** | Clear about their own work, but doesn't adapt to the listener | Explains well when asked; doesn't structure it for the audience | Structures their explanation for the audience; writes code a new teammate could pick up; gives and receives feedback constructively in the live discussion | Makes the people around them clearer: draws others in, explains trade-offs to non-engineers, disagrees well |
| **Curiosity & growth** | Up to date in their own stack, nothing beyond it | Learns outside their lane, but it stays with them | Drives improvement beyond their role; has mentored or influenced others; connects their work to a larger system or goal | Has changed how a team or organisation works because of something they learned, and can show the result |

## Three questions, each mapped to a row

| Question | Scored against |
|---|---|
| Something you're proud of. | Curiosity & growth |
| Something that didn't go to plan. | Technical judgment |
| How you handle disagreement. | Communication & collaboration |

Score the answer, not the story's ending. A failure told well, with what they changed afterwards, is a strong answer.
