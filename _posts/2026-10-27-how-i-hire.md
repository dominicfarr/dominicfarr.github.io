My first hiring was at Informa, and I hired the way I'd been hired. List the technologies, grill the candidate, find someone who knew the APIs. I used to ask candidates to list the Hibernate API and define each method. It was a retention game, not a test of understanding.

That was fourteen years and about 50 hires ago. This series is how I hire now, and how I got here.

## How it changed

There was no single moment. When I joined Policy Expert there was no hiring in the first year. As the business grew we needed more people, and we started off with the same pattern I'd always used. We didn't have a plan.

What we did have was a habit of getting together regularly to reflect. Out of those sessions, hiring turned into something we learned. We'd try something, look honestly at how it went, and change it.

We read what others were doing too. Jeff Atwood's [Why Can't Programmers.. Program?](https://blog.codinghorror.com/why-cant-programmers-program/) passed on Imran Ghory's FizzBuzz, a task so small that anyone who can code should finish it in a couple of minutes. Ghory found most of the graduates he interviewed couldn't. So we tried it. It stopped being useful quickly: people came prepared, and the test told us nothing.

We moved to a kata instead, the shopping cart. Everyone knows a shopping cart. Add and remove items, calculate a price, apply a discount and tax. But the design can go many ways, and everyone takes it slightly differently. It's also good to refactor and extend, so in the face-to-face session we'd pair on a new business rule. That was most weeks in the job anyway: evolving a solution a step at a time.

The questions changed with it. We stopped asking whether someone knew a framework's API and started asking why dependency injection helps testing, whether they'd paired before, what they'd read recently. Clean code, SOLID, immutability, YAGNI, refactoring. Google's head of people, Laszlo Bock, said much the same in 2013: brainteasers "don't predict anything", and what works is "structured behavioural interviews, where you have a consistent rubric" ([ABC News](https://abcnews.go.com/blogs/business/2013/06/google-skips-waste-of-time-brainteaser-interview-questions)).

That's where the idea running through this series comes from: **hire curious learners with strong engineering practices and principles.**

## What came of it

At Policy Expert we grew engineering from 6 to 40 people across 7 teams. Developer retention ran at 3× the London average. Hiring offshore and outside London, and no longer paying recruiter fees of around 30%, saved roughly $400K a year. More important, the team kept delivering while onboarding much higher numbers.

Many of the people I've hired have gone on to senior, principal and head-of roles. I champion people even when they go. It's a natural part of running a team and it always leaves a gap, but if you've done all you can for someone, you can hold your head up high.

## Small teams too

Many of the startup I've worked with were low on funding, grants, friends and family, and my own money, so there was little to offer in pay. We hired graduates and part-timers on small salaries or equity. Hiring there was mostly selling the idea and the opportunity, with training, coaching and mentoring as the hook. The principle held: find people whose curiosity matches what you need.
